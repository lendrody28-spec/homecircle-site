What to do in an overdose

What to do (DRSABCD)

  1. Danger. Make sure you, them and everyone nearby are safe.
  2. Response. Loudly say their name and squeeze their shoulder. If they don't respond, don't assume they're just asleep.
  3. Send for help. Call 000 and say it's an overdose. Don't leave them.
  4. Airway and Breathing. If they're breathing, roll them onto their side in the recovery position and stay with them.
  5. CPR. If they're not breathing normally, start CPR as the 000 operator tells you.
  6. Defibrillation. If someone can bring a defibrillator (AED), attach it and follow its voice prompts.
  7. Naloxone. If opioids might be involved and you have naloxone, use it as the packet or pharmacist instructs. It's free from participating pharmacies and services.
  8. Stay with them until the ambulance arrives.
  9. Tell the paramedics what they took, how much and when, if you know.
More info

Recovery position (St John Ambulance Australia): with them on their back, put their far arm out from their body and their near arm across their chest. Bend their near leg so the foot is on the floor. Roll them away from you onto their side, supporting their head and neck. Keep the bent knee on the ground so they don't roll onto their face, and put their hand under their chin to keep the airway open.

Open windows and loosen tight clothing so they get enough air.

Don't worry about getting in trouble for drug use. healthdirect says paramedics don't need to involve the police unless they feel in danger, and they're focused on saving lives.

Signs of an overdose

They don't need to have every sign. If you're worried, call 000.

More info

Opioids (like heroin, oxycodone, codeine or fentanyl)

  • Can't be woken, or awake but can't respond
  • Slow, shallow or irregular breathing, or not breathing
  • Choking, gurgling or snoring sounds
  • Blue lips or fingertips
  • Pale, cool or clammy skin
  • Very small pupils

Alcohol poisoning

  • Slowed breathing
  • Confusion
  • Blurred vision
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Passing out

Stimulants (like speed, ice, cocaine or MDMA)

  • Chest pain or a racing heartbeat
  • Difficulty breathing
  • Hot, flushed or sweaty skin
  • Severe agitation, paranoia, confusion or panic
  • Severe stomach pain
  • Seizures (fits)

GHB

  • Can't be woken
  • Confused, agitated or hallucinating
  • Sweating or vomiting
  • Slow or irregular breathing
  • Slow or irregular heartbeat
  • Seizures

What not to do

  • Don't give them food or drink.
  • Don't make them vomit.
  • Don't leave them alone, or assume they're just sleeping it off.
  • Don't rely on a cold shower or coffee. They won't sober someone up.

Afterwards

They still need medical care, even if they wake up. Naloxone wears off, and they can overdose again. Always call 000, even if you've given naloxone.

More info

When they wake up, some people feel upset or irritable. Stay calm and keep them company until the ambulance arrives.

If you're worried someone has taken the wrong medicine or too much, but they're awake and well, call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26, any time, anywhere in Australia. If they've collapsed or aren't breathing, call 000.

Free naloxone and first aid courses

Naloxone is free, with no prescription, from participating pharmacies and services under the Australian Government's Take Home Naloxone program. Ask your pharmacist how to use it.

Where to get free naloxone
More info

Naloxone only works on opioids. It won't reverse alcohol or other drug overdoses, but the Alcohol and Drug Foundation says it won't harm someone who hasn't taken an opioid.

A first aid course teaches you CPR and the recovery position hands-on. St John Ambulance runs nationally recognised courses in every state and territory.
